# Change in law firms — Week 9 reference note

Why legal change management is not general change management with different
nouns. Drawn primarily from Rogers and Bell's interview study of change
managers working in law, together with the structural features of partnership
practice.

Index this document to the knowledge base.

## The professional-services problem

Generic change models assume a hierarchy in which a decision, once made, is
implemented. A partnership is not that. The people who must change are also
the owners, the revenue producers and, in many cases, the individuals with the
authority to ignore the decision without consequence.

Rogers and Bell's change managers report the practical consequences.

**Lawyers require evidence.** Participants describe lawyers' habitual
rationality and scepticism as the defining feature of the audience — that data
was needed, and that assertion does not move them. This cuts both ways. It
makes launch rhetoric useless, and it makes a well-evidenced case unusually
durable, because a convinced sceptic is the most credible advocate available:
everyone knows they were hard to convince.

**Autonomy is the operating assumption.** Professional autonomy is not a
cultural quirk; it is close to the definition of the role. Change presented as
a constraint on judgement will be resisted on grounds the profession considers
legitimate — and often correctly so.

**Time is the binding constraint.** Change work is unbillable. Every hour spent
on it is an hour that does not count towards the measure the person is assessed
on. This is not reluctance; it is arithmetic.

**Change managers occupy an ambiguous position.** They usually lack positional
power over the people they must influence, and are often classed as "business
services" rather than fee earners. Their effectiveness depends on borrowed
credibility, which is why the guiding coalition must contain lawyers and be led
by one.

## The structural barriers, named

Any change plan for a law firm should state which of these it is up against
and what it does about each. A plan that does not is relying on goodwill to
defeat an incentive system, which is a bet that has a known outcome.

| Barrier | Mechanism | What it does to a change plan |
|---|---|---|
| Billable-hour targets | Time spent changing is time not counted | The pilot slips, permanently, and nobody refuses |
| Realisation and write-offs | Efficiency can reduce recorded revenue on fixed-scope work | The people best placed to improve delivery are penalised for doing so |
| Annual profit distribution | The horizon for investment is one financial year | Multi-year change competes against this year's distribution |
| Lateral hiring and mobility | Individual reputation is portable; firm process is not | Investment in firm-specific ways of working has an uncertain payback |
| Precedent as a value | Doing it the way it has always been done is a professional virtue in one domain, and gets applied to another | "Innovation" reads as "untested" |
| Client conservatism | Clients ask about change, then buy on relationship and price | Change is deprioritised at the exact moment it is most defensible |

None of these are irrational. Each exists because it solved a real problem.
That is what makes them hard.

## What the change managers say works

- **Start where the pain already is.** Adoption in the group that is currently
  suffering is far cheaper than adoption in the group that is comfortable.
- **Make the first step small and reversible.** Sceptics will accept an
  experiment they can stop; they will not accept a commitment.
- **Show, do not tell.** Evidence from a peer matter beats any presentation.
- **Get a lawyer to say it.** The same words from a fee earner and from a
  business-services manager do not land the same way, and pretending otherwise
  wastes the message.
- **Do not fight the incentive system in public and lose.** Either get the
  measure changed, or scope the change so it does not require people to act
  against their own assessment. Both are legitimate. Ignoring the conflict is
  not.

## What this means for the NexaCare exercise

The change you are proposing this week — implementing the client "happy path"
designed in Week 8 — asks fee earners to record time contemporaneously, to
update task status, and to follow a defined sequence of client touchpoints.

Every one of those asks is small. Collectively they change what a lawyer's
Tuesday looks like, they make individual performance visible in a way it
currently is not, and they cost time that is not billable. Treat the size of
the ask as *behavioural*, not technical. The system change is trivial; the
behaviour change is not.

The evidence that the current state is failing is already in the K&S database.
Find it before you argue about it.
