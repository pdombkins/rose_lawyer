## Obligations engaged by legal project management

Drawing on Rogers and Dombkins (2021), the argument is that LPM generally
*supports* professional obligations rather than threatening them. The
interesting cases are where it inverts.

| Theme | Instruments | LPM's contribution | Where it inverts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Competence, efficiency, access | ASCR r 4; LPUL s 173 | Planning, cost control, resource allocation and risk management support delivering services competently, diligently and as promptly as reasonably possible, and avoiding unnecessary delay that increases costs | A plan built for speed can become the justification for a lower standard of care |
| Client interests, empowerment, fair costs | LPUL ss 3, 172; ASCR r 7 | Strategic and detailed planning, tracking and reporting reduce information asymmetry and support informed client choice about services and costs | The estimate becomes a target, and the target creates pressure to write down time or absorb scope silently |
| Communication, disclosure, confidentiality, privilege | ASCR rr 7, 9 | Encourages communication that is timely, responsive and complete | Status reporting can be optimistic; collaboration platforms expand the confidentiality surface; in-house lawyers in non-legal project roles raise privilege questions |
| Collegiality, diversity, wellbeing | ASCR r 37; Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth); WHS Act 2011 (Cth) | Self-managing teams with better trust, morale and effectiveness, less conflict and rework; diverse teams are more creative | Standardisation can erode the individual lawyer's exercise of independent judgement |

### The challenge scenarios

- Should a lawyer undertake out-of-scope work to deliver a successful outcome?
- Should a lawyer deliver lower-quality work when directed to?
- Should clients be charged for legal project management?
- Does the client understand the scope they actually need?
- Are estimates accurate, and are lawyers writing down time to meet unrealistic ones?
- Are risks, effort or costs under-played to secure work?
- Watermelon reporting — green outside, red inside — is the most commonly
  reported ethical breach by project managers.
- Does the benefit of online collaboration justify the data-disclosure risk?
- Is privilege waived where in-house lawyers undertake non-legal roles on
  non-legal projects?
- Does increased standardisation reduce lawyers' capacity and responsibility
  for independent professional judgement?

## Generative AI in the courts

Positions taken across the Federal Court's GPN-AI, Practice Note SC Gen 23, the
District Court's General Practice Note 2, and the Law Society of NSW statement
and *A Solicitor's Guide to Responsible Use of AI*.

**Contemplated as permissible**

- Administrative tasks such as drafting and summarising documents
- Generating chronologies, indexes and witness lists
- Preparing briefs and draft Crown Case Statements
- Summarising or reviewing documents and transcripts
- Preparing written submissions or summaries of arguments, **with source verification**
- Using closed-source AI with confidentiality controls for proceeding-related information

**Prohibited**

- Entering prohibited or confidential information into uncontrolled and unrestricted generative AI
- Generating affidavits, witness statements, character references or other evidentiary material
- Using generative AI to alter or rephrase written witness evidence
- Drafting or preparing an expert report without prior leave of the court
- Preparing written submissions without manually verifying all citations

The catalogue of cases in which fabricated authorities reached a court is
maintained at damiencharlotin.com.

## The third line of defence: professional standards

Beyond the tool and the firm sits the set of obligations that bind the
individual lawyer and that no firm policy can waive on their behalf:

competence, integrity and honesty (ASCR r 4) · fair and reasonable costs
(LPUL s 172) · confidentiality (ASCR r 9) · avoidance of personal bias
(ASCR r 17) · duty to the court (ASCR r 19) · supervision of legal services
(ASCR r 37).

The three questions the week poses:

1. Should AI be able to provide legal advice — and if not, how do you define
   "legal advice" in a way that is workable in practice?
2. What are the ethical and commercial implications of using AI and other
   automated systems in legal practice?
3. Can hallucinations make for good law?

## Ethical judgement in the age of AI

Legg (2025) argues that AI raises rather than removes the demand for ethical
judgement.

- **Ethical fading** — the ethical dimension of a decision disappears from view
  when it is framed as commercial, technical or administrative. The language
  does the work: "efficiency", "commercial reality", "that's just how it's
  recorded", "the client is comfortable with it".
- **Automation bias** — a confident, fluent output invites acceptance
  precisely because it is confident and fluent.
- **Deskilling** — judgement is developed by doing the work. Where the first
  draft always arrives already formed, the junior lawyer may never build the
  judgement needed to evaluate it.
- **Role morality** — the lawyer's professional obligations are not the same as
  their personal morality, and are also not the same as the client's moral
  position. The overriding duty to the court and the administration of justice
  sits above both.
- **Practical wisdom** — drawing on Kronman and the Aristotelian tradition,
  the capacity to deliberate well about what should be done in a particular
  case, which is not reducible to rule-following.
- **Reflective practice** — the prescription. Deliberately processing an
  experience: what happened, what was done, what could have been done
  differently, and what will change next time. This is the mechanism by which
  experience becomes judgement rather than merely accumulating.

## Minimising complaints

The Office of the NSW Legal Services Commissioner identifies these as the
practical drivers of complaints:

- Effective record keeping and file notes
- Checklists for active files, and conflict of interest checks for new matters
- Regular communication to understand the client's values and priorities
- Managing expectations — explaining delays, process changes, cost increases and scope
- Prompt responses to client queries and concerns
- Calls to the practice answered politely and professionally
- Clear costs disclosure

Compare this list against the CX lifecycle. Almost every item is a
communication or expectation-management practice rather than a legal one.
